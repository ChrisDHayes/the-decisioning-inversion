# The Decisioning Inversion
### When intelligence moves, who gets to decide?

**An interactive research essay by Christopher Hayes, CDAIO · MPS DAV · MBAN · MSc in AI (Boston University candidate)**

We built organizations to coordinate human intelligence. What changes when artificial intelligence can contribute across many stages of a decision—but does not necessarily inherit the authority, judgment, or accountability of the people and institutions involved?

This project combines a **five-act scrollytelling experience**, a research paper, a Substack essay, and five original conceptual frameworks. It is written for a broad audience, using a Canadian wealth management dealer as one concrete example of a more general problem.

**[Open the interactive experience](https://ChrisDHayes.github.io/the-decisioning-inversion/)** · *GitHub Pages must be enabled in Settings → Pages before the site URL becomes live.*

## Begin with the experience

The root [`index.html`](./index.html) is the **locked V3.2 Broad Audience Edition**, preserved without modification.

1. **The inherited institution:** why organizations have handoffs, expertise, supervision, and authority.
2. **Distributed intelligence:** where AI can contribute within a larger decision system.
3. **Coordination and productive friction:** when connecting information helps—and when common errors spread.
4. **The authority boundary:** why an AI recommendation is not permission to act.
5. **Evidence and learning:** how to ask why a decision happened and whether it was justified.

The laboratory uses synthetic scenarios and allows readers to configure AI contributions, replay decisions, introduce faults, and explore the consequences of independent challenge and bounded authority.

## Evidence discipline

The HTML is a **deterministic, rule-based teaching model**: it does not use real clients, portfolio data, or trades. Its events, flags, evidence origins, permissions, and outcomes are **synthetic**, not measured control effectiveness or empirical proof of a governance design. The academic and regulatory sources support the *surrounding propositions*; they do not validate the simulated outcomes. Regulatory obligations depend on the institution, registration, account type, mandate, and context. This work is not investment, legal, or regulatory advice.
